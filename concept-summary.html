Quiet Neurons: a BDH quietens on what it just learned, not on what is merely predictable

DataForge 2026, Pathway track · concept Sparse Non-Negative Activations · Nilay Toshniwal, solo · artifact nilaymastaadmi.github.io/quiet-neurons · code github.com/nilaymastaadmi/quiet-neurons

The design pressure

A dense Transformer evaluates every feed-forward unit for every token, hard or not; activation sparsity is the counter-proposal. But Transformer sparsity is already emergent, arriving with training rather than by design [3]; it needs a hard rectifier, because only that yields exact zeros [4]; and which units fire is input-dependent, not merely how many [5]. Sparsity alone therefore distinguishes nothing. What distinguishes an architecture is what its sparsity is a function of.

What BDH changes

Dragon Hatchling (BDH) [1] makes it structural. In the BDH-GPU formulation Pathway open-sources, every layer multiplies two rectified (ReLU) vectors elementwise, and a product of two sparse non-negative vectors is sparser than either. Active here means a strictly non-zero entry in that product; a rectifier cannot emit a negative, so an active neuron is a positive amount of something rather than a signed coordinate, which is what lets one be read as a concept at all. Attention is the second change: query and key are the same tensor, the code asserting K is Q, so the score matrix is a causally-masked Gram matrix of neuron activations with no softmax. Positions whose neurons fired alike bind to each other: the paper's Hebbian reading, in one line of tensor code.

The claim, and the correction it needed

Section 6.4 reports that "fewer neurons are active ... for more predictable input signals": at layer 2 of a 65,536-neuron model, 4.0–7.5% active while memorising a new word against about 2.5% while repeating it, a 1.6–3.0× ratio [1, Fig. 14]. We reproduced that protocol on three models trained here from the public code, and it survives a 32-fold shrink: 1.43×, 1.97× and 1.87× at 2,048, 8,192 and 16,384 neurons (8.24/5.76%, 7.18/3.64% and 8.20/4.38% memorising against repeating), each averaged over 1,280 sequences across five pinned samples, spread near 0.005. In aggregate the paper is right: predictable text is quieter. What follows sharpens that rather than contradicting it.

The same measurement narrows what the result can mean. Two blocks of one sequence are both predicted almost perfectly yet differ two and a half fold in activity: the 13-letter warm-up runs at 9.1% of layer 2 active at 0.0005 nats of mean surprise (a nat is the natural-log unit of cross-entropy; zero is a perfect prediction), the 56 repeated letters at 3.6% at 0.008 nats, and 3.9× apart at the extremes. The warm-up is identical in every training sequence, so it lives in the weights; the repeated word is new each sequence, so it can only come from the context. What separates them is not predictability but where the knowledge came from. We show that activity tracks that; we do not show why. A learner falsifies it in a minute: inject an unpredictable letter mid-repetition and activity rises there, +10% to +20% by word, while the letter before is unchanged to nine decimals, because the model reads only leftwards.

Evidence, labelled

Reproduced here: the three ratios, the counterexample, the surprise-injection check, and parity of the in-browser model against PyTorch (logit max difference 4.1e-5, eleven of twelve sparsity series bit-identical). Developer-reported, not reproduced: the Figure 14 band; 97.4% on Sudoku Extreme, which Pathway's own repository states comes from their internal implementation and does not reproduce from the open code; 1B-to-600B pretraining-scaling experiments; and BDH-CQ's 29.5% pass@2 on ARC-AGI-1 at $0.0007 per task [2]. Not modelled: BDH-CQ's internals, whose "dimensions, exact update rules, and implementation details remain proprietary" [2]. BDH-CQ has no direct role in this concept; inventing one would be worse than saying so. The strongest external grounding Pathway offers is development on Amazon SageMaker HyperPod, a commercial partnership rather than an independent evaluation; no external reproduction of the headline results has been published. Where it is likely worse: sparse non-negative activations plausibly cost representational capacity against the dense superposition a Transformer uses, and no published comparison runs past GPT-2 scale.

Limitations, maturity, and what would settle them

The effect is not uniform with depth: layer 0 runs backwards at all three sizes (0.82, 0.79, 0.86) and layer 3 is flat below 16,384 neurons, weakly positive (1.11×) there. Scaling is not monotonic — 16,384 measures below 8,192 by twenty times the sampling spread — and we published the opposite before the third model finished. One confound stands: a single 4,000-step schedule cut by wall clock at 1,854, 1,917 and 2,309 steps, so close final losses (0.176, 0.175, 0.172) mean comparably trained, not controlled. These are toys, 0.4M to 6.3M parameters on a synthetic task, as the paper's own protocol is. Surprise and sparsity do not track per letter (8,192 neurons, layer 2: Pearson 0.29, Spearman 0.02), which killed a stronger claim. We count the elementwise product, the only quantity in the public code reaching the paper's band, and the paper does not say which tensor it means; that changes the effect's size, not its direction. Most importantly we establish the signature, not its cause: nothing here explains why context-held knowledge needs fewer neurons than weight-held knowledge, and the control that would settle it relocates identical content between weights and context while holding everything else fixed. Maturity: credible and outsider-checkable for this narrow property; the wider programme is earlier, its headline benchmarks developer-reported.
